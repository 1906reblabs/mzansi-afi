§ 01
Executive Brief

South Africa's insurers are this month handing their regulator something the FSCA has never had before: a single, standardised, machine-readable risk profile, submitted through the OMNI-Risk Return and fed into the live Integrated Regulatory Solution (IRS), scheduled for full go-live in September 2026. The Return applies to every FSCA-regulated institution — insurers, retirement funds, advisers, administrators — and its deadline does not wait for the COFI Bill, still only at an earlier stage in Parliament.

Why it matters this month: the go-live lands in the same week the Board of Healthcare Funders, representing roughly half of South Africa's medical scheme beneficiaries, formally challenged the Council for Medical Schemes' 2027 contribution guidance — a public dispute that exists because the CMS, unlike the FSCA from this month, has no systemic way to see which schemes are exceeding its guidance in aggregate before one reaches curatorship, as Sizwe Hosmed did in September 2025.

Who wins, who loses: insurers that have already built the governance processes this publication has described across Issues 006 through 008 — assumption-challenge panels, vendor-dependency mapping, documented AI explainability — are submitting a Return that reflects real discipline. Insurers filling in the Return as a data-mapping exercise are handing the FSCA an accurate picture of exactly the gaps this publication has documented, before doing anything to close them.

Black Swan Watch
Tail risks the industry is systematically underestimating
The IRS Go-Live Nobody Has Rehearsed Regulatory
Timeline: Short — already materialising
The Underestimation Argument

The FSCA published the draft OMNI-Risk Return and Explanatory Guide on 30 September 2025 under Communication 19 of 2025, ran a two-month consultation to 30 November 2025, and scheduled an industry pilot for mid-2026 ahead of a full IRS go-live in September 2026 — this month. The Return replaces the old Omni-Conduct of Business Return and is structured across twelve sections covering group structure and beneficial ownership, geographical footprint, governance (board composition, remuneration, non-compliance tolerance, insurance and guarantee claims), customer base and politically exposed persons, and more. Industry commentary through 2026 treated the consultation and pilot phases as the main event; the actual go-live, arriving now, has drawn comparatively little coverage relative to its supervisory significance — every regulated institution is handing the FSCA a comparable, standardised risk profile for the first time in the regulator's history.

The Nonlinear Impact Scenario

An insurer's OMNI-Risk Return — submitted without the internal governance review this publication has recommended since Issue 006 — feeds into the IRS's automated risk model and generates a supervisory flag the institution did not anticipate, because nobody inside the business modelled what the twelve sections would show in aggregate before submitting them separately, department by department. The FSCA's supervisory attention, no longer dependent on a specific incident crystallising as it was with Banxso, turns to the flagged institution on the strength of its own governance data.

Fragility Amplifiers

The Return explicitly captures governance non-compliance tolerance and remediation history, alongside insurance and guarantee claims data at board level — meaning institutions that have treated governance documentation as a retrospective exercise, rather than a contemporaneous one, are submitting a comparatively weaker picture of themselves than institutions that have not.

Antifragility Opportunity

An insurer that runs its own internal OMNI-Risk Return simulation before the formal submission — modelling what its twelve-section aggregate profile will show a regulator seeing it for the first time — can identify and address governance gaps on its own timeline rather than the FSCA's.

The CMS Guidance Nobody Is Bound By Regulatory
Timeline: Short — live now
The Underestimation Argument

The Council for Medical Schemes' Circular 20 of 2026, published 31 July 2026, recommends that 2027 contribution increases and cost assumptions be anchored at 3.8%, based on the SARB's July 2026 CPI forecast. The anchor is guidance, not a cap — trustees may go higher with justification to the Registrar — and recent history shows most do: the industry-average increase for 2025 was 10.1% against a projected CPI of 3.0%, and multiple major schemes' 2026 increases ranged from 6.76% to 9.9% against a 3.3%-plus-utilisation guidance figure. Around 22–23 September 2026, the Board of Healthcare Funders formally wrote to the CMS Registrar challenging the 2027 guidance as unrealistic and calling for structural reform, including implementation of the Competition Commission's Health Market Inquiry recommendations on tariff negotiation, rather than guidance alone. The industry is treating this as an annual ritual dispute; it is better read as evidence that CMS guidance, absent a systemic enforcement mechanism, functions as a public statement of intent rather than a binding constraint.

The Nonlinear Impact Scenario

2027 contribution increases land, once again, materially above the CMS's 3.8% anchor across most major open schemes. The CMS has no equivalent to the FSCA's incoming IRS — no standardised, cross-scheme, machine-readable risk profile that would let it see, before increases are gazetted, which schemes are both exceeding guidance and eroding solvency reserves simultaneously. The pattern that preceded Sizwe Hosmed's September 2025 statutory curatorship repeats at another scheme, and is again visible to the regulator only in hindsight.

Fragility Amplifiers

The BHF's own letter argues that guidance alone cannot address the underlying cost drivers, implicitly acknowledging that the CMS's primary lever — an annual circular — has no automated visibility mechanism behind it comparable to what the FSCA is switching on this month for the rest of the financial sector.

Antifragility Opportunity

A scheme administrator or broker that builds and publishes its own comparable, standardised solvency-and-contribution disclosure — ahead of any CMS requirement to do so — captures the same first-mover credibility advantage this publication described for FSCA-regulated insurers building bias-governance disclosures in Issue 006.

The Reinsurance Gap That Widened Instead of Closing Macro
Timeline: Short — live at every renewal date
The Underestimation Argument

This publication flagged in Issue 008 that South Africa-specific reinsurance conditions, as described by EY's local outlook, appeared to be hardening even as every reported global renewal in 2026 showed softening. Since then, the global softening has intensified rather than reversed: Gallagher Re described January 2027 as shaping up as one of the strongest buyer's markets in a decade, citing record reinsurance capital and a 19.9% half-year return on equity; Moody's, S&P and Fitch have each maintained a stable-to-deteriorating sector outlook driven by falling property-catastrophe pricing following more than 20% cumulative declines since 2024; and AM Best/Marsh Re data puts dedicated global reinsurance capital ending 2026 at $705 billion, up from $663 billion at year-end 2025. No source reviewed for this issue has published a South Africa-specific renewal benchmark reconciling the local and global pictures.

The Nonlinear Impact Scenario

A South African primary insurer enters January 2027 renewal negotiations citing the global softening data as leverage, without an independent, locally-sourced benchmark to confirm whether African catastrophe capacity, cedant-specific loss experience or genuinely divergent local market dynamics explain the gap EY described — and negotiates from an unverified premise in either direction, for the second consecutive renewal cycle this publication has tracked.

Fragility Amplifiers

Reinsurers globally are reportedly showing little appetite to give back attachment points and terms secured in 2023 even as headline prices fall — meaning a South African cedant that wins on price without independently verifying structure and retention terms may be trading a smaller, visible gain for a larger, less visible one.

Antifragility Opportunity

The insurer or broker that obtains and publishes even a directional South Africa-specific renewal benchmark — the opportunity this publication first identified in Issue 008 and which remains uncaptured — now has two consecutive renewal cycles of global data to benchmark against, making the exercise more valuable, not less, the longer it goes unbuilt.

What Santam's Own Balance Sheet Is Saying About Growth at Home Market
Timeline: Short — already materialising
The Underestimation Argument

Santam, Africa's largest general insurer, paused its planned US expansion in early September 2026, citing a decline in US property premium rates, redirecting instead toward reinsurance capacity at India's Gujarat International Finance Tec-City (GIFT City) and a newly created Executive Head of Pan-Africa and International role — appointed 14 September 2026 — focused on the Middle East, India and South-East Asia. Santam's international business expanded 25% in the six months to June 2026, against 6% growth in its South African business over the same period, and the insurer, which still generates 77% of its premiums domestically, has stated an ambition for international operations to reach 30% of the total by 2030. Trade coverage has largely framed these moves as routine international expansion; read together, they are South Africa's largest general insurer's own capital-allocation decisions, stating more plainly than any market commentary where it currently sees growth.

The Nonlinear Impact Scenario

Santam's 6% South African growth rate, if representative of the broader personal and commercial lines market rather than company-specific, implies the domestic short-term insurance market is growing well below the pace its own premium-rate and claims-inflation pressures — documented across this publication's prior issues — would suggest is needed simply to hold combined ratios steady, meaning the market's real growth problem may be demand-side, not only a claims-cost or fraud problem.

Fragility Amplifiers

Santam's pivot toward India and the Middle East follows a January 2026 reinsurance branch licence at GIFT City — the first insurer from a BRICS country to establish there, according to the company — meaning the capital and executive attention behind the shift is not exploratory but already structurally committed.

Antifragility Opportunity

A competitor or broker that reads Santam's public capital-allocation signal accurately — rather than only its marketing narrative — can identify which specific South African segments Santam itself judges to be growth-constrained, and target underwriting or advisory capacity at the adjacent segments Santam is not prioritising.

I
The OMNI-Risk Return Readiness Premium
Modelling your own supervisory risk score before the regulator does
The Conventional View
The OMNI-Risk Return is being treated internally, where it is discussed at all, as a data-mapping and systems exercise — assembling twelve sections' worth of governance, ownership, geographic and customer data into the FSCA's required format, owned by compliance and IT rather than by risk or the executive committee.
The Actual Economics
The Return is the input to the FSCA's new automated supervisory risk model under the IRS, going live this month — the first time the regulator will hold a single, standardised, comparable risk profile for every institution it supervises, rather than fragmented, licence-by-licence assessments. An institution that runs its own internal simulation of what its aggregate twelve-section profile will show — before submitting it — can identify and close governance gaps documented informally across this publication's last three issues (anchored renewal pricing, AI vendor concentration, absent NHI transition protocols, board-level non-compliance tolerance) on its own timeline, rather than discovering them via an FSCA-initiated review the Return itself triggered.
Why It Stays Hidden
The advantage is invisible from outside the institution — no competitor can observe whether a rival has run an internal readiness simulation before submitting, and the FSCA has given no indication it will publish comparative OMNI-Risk Return scores.
Who Is Best Positioned
An insurer or group with existing risk-governance infrastructure — an assumption-challenge process, a documented AI-bias-testing protocol, a vendor-dependency map — of the kind this publication has recommended building since Issue 006 has the readiness simulation largely built already; it simply needs to be pointed at the Return's twelve sections rather than treated as new work.
The Risk
Once the IRS has been live for a full supervisory cycle and institutions understand empirically what triggers a flagged review, the first-mover readiness advantage compresses into a standard compliance step that every institution performs before submission — likely within twelve to eighteen months, consistent with the erosion pattern this publication has documented for comparable governance-disclosure windows.
II
The 2027 Contribution Gap Advisory
Quantifying, for corporate HR clients, the distance between CMS guidance and likely reality
The Conventional View
Employee-benefits brokers present the CMS's annual contribution-increase guidance to corporate clients as a useful planning anchor, updating clients when schemes announce their actual increases closer to the new benefit year.
The Actual Economics
The CMS's own guidance history is now well-documented and publicly contested: the 2025 industry-average increase landed at 10.1% against a 3.0% guidance-linked CPI projection; 2026 increases across major schemes ranged from 6.76% to 9.9% against 3.3%-plus-utilisation guidance; and the Board of Healthcare Funders has now formally challenged the CMS's 3.8% anchor for 2027 as unrealistic before a single scheme has announced its actual figure. A broker who quantifies this documented gap for corporate HR clients now — presenting a realistic 2027 planning range rather than the CMS anchor alone — is providing evidenced, differentiated advice rather than repeating a guidance figure with a multi-year track record of being exceeded.
Why It Stays Hidden
The gap is visible only to someone who reads past the CMS circular headline to the BHF's own analysis and the multi-year increase history; most corporate HR budget planning cycles work directly from the published guidance figure.
Who Is Best Positioned
Employee-benefits brokers and gap-cover underwriters with existing corporate HR relationships, who can combine the documented guidance-versus-reality gap with the medical scheme benefit-comparison and gap-cover advisory capability this publication has recommended in prior issues.
The Risk
If the CMS's 2027 guidance turns out, unusually, to hold close to 3.8% — a first in the multi-year pattern this publication has documented — brokers who presented a materially higher planning range lose credibility on the specific figure, even if the underlying advisory service remains sound.

§ 06
Second-Order Effects
The causal chain beyond the obvious headline
This Month's Trend
The FSCA's Integrated Regulatory Solution (IRS), built on the OMNI-Risk Return, reaches full go-live in September 2026 — replacing fragmented, licence-by-licence supervisory assessments with a single, standardised, machine-readable risk profile for every regulated financial institution, including every insurer, applicable regardless of the COFI Bill's parliamentary progress.
First-Order Effects — Obvious (0-6 months)
  1. The FSCA published the draft OMNI-Risk Return and Explanatory Guide on 30 September 2025 under Communication 19 of 2025, opening a two-month public consultation to 30 November 2025.
  2. Three virtual workshops covering all twelve sections of the draft Return ran through November 2025, with FSCA supervisors asking institutions to flag data-availability hurdles and sector-specific nuances.
  3. An industry pilot of the Return ran in mid-2026, ahead of the scheduled full IRS go-live this month.
  4. Compliance and legal commentary through 2026 has framed the Return primarily as a reporting-format change — a successor to the old Omni-Conduct of Business Return — requiring data mapping and systems work.
  5. The Return's deadline applies independently of the COFI Bill's own parliamentary timeline, which remains at an earlier legislative stage.
Second-Order Effects — Non-Obvious (6-24 months)

The non-obvious consequence is what changes about how the FSCA actually supervises, once the Return is live rather than in consultation. A licence-by-licence review model depends on someone — a complainant, an examiner, an incident — directing supervisory attention to a specific institution; the Banxso enforcement action this publication analysed in Issue 008 is a clean example of that model working as designed, once a specific incident crystallised the risk. An automated, comparable risk model built on standardised data from every regulated institution does not need that trigger. It can, in principle, flag institutions whose governance, group-structure or customer-outcome data looks anomalous relative to their peers, before any specific incident occurs — converting supervision from reactive to comparative.

A second consequence concerns the Council for Medical Schemes specifically, which this issue's Fragility Index and Category Intelligence both flag as the clearest current example of a regulator without an equivalent tool. The BHF's September 2026 challenge to CMS guidance exists in a supervisory environment where the CMS's only real lever — an annual circular — cannot see, in aggregate and in advance, which of the registered schemes are both exceeding guidance and eroding solvency reserves simultaneously. The FSCA's move to automated, comparative supervision this month sharpens the contrast: within the same broader financial-sector supervisory architecture, one regulator is modernising toward exactly the systemic visibility the other publicly, structurally, lacks.

A third consequence runs through the fronting and vendor-concentration risks this publication flagged across Issues 006 and 008. The OMNI-Risk Return's group-structure and shared-services sections are among the first regulatory instruments with the structural potential to make cross-institution vendor and fronting dependencies visible in standardised form — though whether the FSCA's automated model is actually built to detect correlated risk across nominally separate institutions, rather than only assess each institution's own data in isolation, is not established by any source reviewed for this issue and remains a genuine open question.

Third-Order Effects — Structural Implications (2-7 years)

If the IRS's automated model proves capable of surfacing genuine governance gaps — anchored pricing, vendor concentration, absent transition planning — before they crystallise into incidents, a bifurcation becomes likely between institutions that used the run-up to go-live to build real governance discipline (the assumption-challenge processes, bias-testing protocols and vendor-dependency maps this publication has recommended since Issue 006) and those that treated the Return as a data-mapping exercise. The former group's OMNI-Risk Return submissions become, in effect, a continuously updated credential; the latter group discovers its own governance gaps at the same moment the regulator does.

The CMS's structural disadvantage, sharpened by this month's contrast, plausibly accelerates pressure — from the BHF, from political actors already engaged in disputes over public-sector medical scheme increases, and from the comparison to FSCA modernisation itself — for the CMS to build a comparable systemic tool. Given the FSCA's own timeline (consultation opened September 2025, pilot mid-2026, go-live September 2026 — roughly a twelve-month build), a comparable CMS capability, if pursued, would plausibly not arrive before 2028, leaving at least one more full annual contribution cycle exposed to the same guidance-without-visibility pattern this issue documents.

The Strategic Pivot Point
does an institution treat the OMNI-Risk Return as a reporting-format change to be completed correctly, or as the first artifact the regulator will hold that lets it compare this institution's governance against every other regulated entity in the country, continuously, without waiting for an incident? The FSCA's own supervisory model is changing this month regardless of which answer any individual institution gives.

Strategic Recommendations
Specific moves available now — informed by this month's intelligence
For Insurers
Simulate Your Own OMNI-Risk Return Before Submitting It
Before completing the formal OMNI-Risk Return, run an internal exercise that assembles all twelve sections' data as the FSCA's automated model will see it in aggregate — group structure, governance non-compliance history, customer base, geographic footprint — rather than allowing each section to be completed independently by the department that holds the underlying data.
Why now: The IRS go-live is this month; an institution that has not modelled its own aggregate profile before submission has no way to know what supervisory attention its own data might attract.
Consolidate COFI-Readiness and OMNI-Risk Return Documentation Into One Programme
Merge the internal teams and documentation currently preparing separately for COFI licensing readiness and OMNI-Risk Return submission into a single governance-disclosure workstream, given the substantial overlap in the underlying evidence — conduct standards, customer-outcome data, governance documentation — both require.
Why now: Treating the two as separate projects, as this publication flagged for AI-governance and COFI documentation in Issue 008, duplicates effort against what is functionally a single underlying supervisory expectation.
Map Fronting and Group-Structure Dependencies Before the Return's Group-Structure Section Does It First
For any insurer operating fronting arrangements with insurtechs or MGAs, or any group with multiple FSCA-regulated licences, proactively document the capital, data and conduct-liability relationships between entities, ahead of submitting the OMNI-Risk Return's group-structure and shared-services sections.
Why now: These sections are specifically designed to surface exactly this kind of cross-entity dependency; an institution that has already mapped and can explain its own structure enters the disclosure from a position of clarity rather than discovery.
For Brokers
Quantify the CMS Guidance-Versus-Reality Gap for Every Corporate Health Benefits Client
For corporate HR clients budgeting for 2027 medical scheme contributions, present a realistic planning range informed by the documented multi-year gap between CMS guidance and actual scheme increases, rather than the CMS's 3.8% anchor for 2027 alone.
Why now: The Board of Healthcare Funders has itself now publicly challenged the 2027 guidance as unrealistic, given the multi-year track record; brokers who present the anchor figure alone are offering less complete advice than the industry's own representative body is currently providing in public.
Prepare Clients for the Reality Behind Global Reinsurance Softening Headlines
For clients whose commercial property or specialty cover pricing references global reinsurance trends, explicitly flag that no South Africa-specific renewal benchmark reconciling the global softening data with EY's description of local hardening conditions has been published across the two renewal cycles this publication has tracked, and advise accordingly.
Why now: January 2027 renewal negotiations are approaching, and Gallagher Re and Moody's both describe global conditions as increasingly buyer-favourable — a characterisation clients may reasonably but wrongly assume applies unmodified to their own African risk placement.
Build an OMNI-Risk-Return-Literate Advisory Offering for Institutional Clients
For brokers and advisers serving institutional or corporate clients that are themselves FSCA-regulated, develop the capability to explain what the OMNI-Risk Return and IRS go-live mean for those clients' own supervisory exposure — a genuinely new advisory need created by this month's regulatory shift.
Why now: The go-live is new and live now, creating a client-education need most brokers have not yet built capability to serve, distinct from more familiar COFI-readiness advisory work.
For Regulators
CMS: Commission a Feasibility Study for a Comparable Systemic Solvency-and-Contribution Visibility Tool
Following the BHF's formal September 2026 challenge to the 2027 contribution guidance, and the precedent of Sizwe Hosmed's September 2025 curatorship, commission a feasibility study — drawing explicitly on the FSCA's OMNI-Risk Return and IRS design process as a reference model — for a standardised, comparable, forward-looking solvency and contribution-assumption disclosure across all registered medical schemes.
Why now: The contrast between the FSCA's live, automated supervisory model and the CMS's guidance-only mechanism is sharper this month than at any point this publication has covered it; a feasibility study started now, using the FSCA's own recently completed build as a template, shortens what would otherwise be a multi-year design process.
FSCA: Publish What the IRS's Automated Model Is and Is Not Designed to Detect
Provide the industry with explicit guidance on whether the OMNI-Risk Return's automated supervisory model is designed to detect correlated, cross-institution risks — such as shared vendor dependencies or common fronting relationships — or is scoped to assess each institution's own submitted data in isolation.
Why now: This issue found no public source clarifying this scope, and the distinction materially affects how institutions should prepare; the FSCA's own November 2025 report with the PA on AI adoption identified fragmented, institution-level governance review as a specific sector weakness the Return could, in principle, help address.
National Treasury: Build a Reinsurance Renewal Disclosure Requirement Into COFI's Reporting Framework
As COFI's activity-based licensing and reporting framework is finalised, include a requirement for primary insurers to disclose aggregate, anonymised renewal pricing movement data, addressing the information gap this publication has now documented across two consecutive renewal cycles with no South Africa-specific benchmark available to the market.
Why now: COFI's own reporting architecture is still being designed, at the explanatory-summary stage; building this requirement in now costs less than retrofitting it after the framework is finalised.
§ 08
The Contrarian Take
Thiel Mode — what important truth does almost nobody in the industry agree with?
⊞ The Consensus Belief Being Challenged
the OMNI-Risk Return is a reporting-format modernisation — a bigger, more structured successor to the old Omni-Conduct of Business Return, requiring data-mapping and systems investment, but not, in itself, a change to what the FSCA actually knows about any given institution beyond what more granular data collection provides.

The contrarian claim: the Return is not a bigger form. It is the first instrument that lets the FSCA compare one insurer's governance quality against every other regulated entity in the country, continuously, without needing a complaint or an incident to direct its attention — converting South African financial supervision from a reactive, complaint-and-incident-driven model, the model that produced the Banxso enforcement action this publication analysed in Issue 008, to a comparative one. Institutions treating the Return as a data-mapping exercise are, in effect, volunteering the evidence for exactly the governance gaps this publication has spent three issues documenting.

The sharpest evidence: the Return's twelve sections explicitly capture governance non-compliance tolerance and remediation history at board level — not merely operational metrics — meaning institutions submit a direct account of their own governance discipline, not a proxy for it. The IRS is designed, in the FSCA's own consultation language, to produce a single, comparable risk profile for each financial institution regardless of how many licences it holds — an explicit framing of cross-institution comparison, not institution-by-institution assessment. And the go-live proceeds on its own September 2026 timeline regardless of where COFI sits in the parliamentary process — the Return is not contingent on COFI's own slower legislative path, meaning institutions cannot treat it as part of a longer, still-negotiable reform horizon.

The strategic implication: every institution that has treated the governance recommendations in this publication's last three issues — assumption-challenge panels, AI bias-testing protocols, vendor-dependency mapping, fronting-relationship documentation — as optional, forward-looking best practice rather than urgent, now has a concrete, dated reason those recommendations were never optional: the regulator is switching on, this month, the first tool capable of seeing whether an institution built them or not.

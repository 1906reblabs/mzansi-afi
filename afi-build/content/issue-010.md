profit_pools:
  - title: "The Soft-Market Aggregate Window"
    subtitle: "Frequency protection priced by a market with record capital"
    conventional_view: >-
      Property catastrophe reinsurance is bought to protect the balance sheet against peak
      events. Frequency losses from heat, fire and crop damage are retained and priced into
      the book, on the assumption that aggregate protection is hard to obtain.
    actual_economics: >-
      Moody's survey of buyers found more than half of primary insurers expect aggregate
      covers to be more available at 1 January 2027. Global property-catastrophe pricing has
      fallen more than 20% in 18 months, Aon expects savings of roughly 10%, and S&P expects
      the soft market to deepen in 2027 regardless of losses. Dedicated
      global reinsurance capital is projected to end 2026 at US$705 billion, up from US$663
      billion, per AM Best and Marsh Re data cited in Issue 009. A strong El Niño raises the
      likelihood of exactly the correlated frequency losses an aggregate cover pools. The
      window is the combination: elevated seasonal risk, signalled by SAWS, and a market
      pricing capacity generously. Renasa's guidance to cedants was to lead renewal
      discussions with exposure data rather than price.
    why_hidden: >-
      The saving shows up as a ceded-premium line, not a profit line, and its value depends
      on a loss season that has not happened. Reinsurers are holding attachment points, so a
      lower price can coexist with a higher retained layer; the net benefit depends on
      structure, which no headline reports.
    best_positioned: >-
      Cedants with granular claims data by peril and district and the capacity to model a heat
      and fire scenario before the 1 January negotiations.
    risk: >-
      SAWS's own caution applies: not every strong El Niño brings drought. If the season
      brings rain-fed rather than drought losses, the cover is bought against a loss that
      does not arrive, and held-firm terms may leave retentions higher than the headline
      saving implies.

  - title: "Municipal Credit Knowledge Before the Guarantee Vehicle"
    subtitle: "An underwriting skill in an exposure the state is about to institutionalise"
    conventional_view: >-
      Municipal credit risk is treated as a government and banking matter. Insurers meet it
      indirectly, through contractors' trade credit and surety, and price it from general
      market data.
    actual_economics: >-
      The 21 October statement supplies the outline of a dataset. Transfers to 69 municipalities
      were suspended in June for persistent non-payment of creditors; by the end of July 37
      had council-approved repayment plans and R4.2 billion had been paid to creditors. A
      Treasury review found that most municipalities cover day-to-day operations but spend too
      much on running costs, leaving too little for long-term infrastructure and
      maintenance. The Credit Guarantee Vehicle, incorporated in August and seeking a
      Prudential Authority insurance licence, aims to mobilise private capital for
      infrastructure, and 31 of the 68 projects in the public-private partnership pipeline
      are municipal. AFI inference: an insurer or surety writer that builds municipal
      payment-behaviour knowledge now holds a skill a state-backed guarantor will also need.
      The Treasury has not said what the vehicle will guarantee in municipal projects.
      AFI inference: smart-meter rollout, which receives R3.5 billion more over the medium term on
      top of R2 billion, would also generate usage and payment data at municipal level that
      insurers do not currently see.
    why_hidden: >-
      Creditor-payment and repayment-plan data sit in Treasury and municipal reporting, not in
      an insurance dataset. The 37-of-69 figure is a July snapshot; the status of the other 32
      is not reported in the sources reviewed, and no current list was located.
    best_positioned: >-
      Surety, credit and construction insurers with existing contractor books in municipal
      work, and the reinsurers behind them.
    risk: >-
      A licensed, state-backed guarantor with development-partner capital competes on price.
      Its product scope is unpublished; if it guarantees lenders rather than contractors, the
      pool open to private insurers is narrower than this entry assumes.

## Executive Brief

National finances have stabilised; the tier beneath them has not. The 21 October Medium-Term Budget Policy Statement confirmed debt-to-GDP stabilised at 78.4% in 2025/26 and a narrower deficit of 3.9%. The same speech records that infrastructure grants of R887 billion produced projects that are often delayed, incomplete or fail to deliver the intended benefits, and that transfers to 69 municipalities were suspended in June for not paying creditors.

**Why it matters this month:** four fixed dates land on that tier within weeks of each other. The repo rate rose to 7.25% on 23 September, after GDP contracted 0.2% in the second quarter. The 4 November local government elections arrive with fuel-levy relief the same day, and Sanlam's 5 October offer for the rest of Santam adds a structural change to the same weeks. SAWS has called the El Niño exceptional in timing and magnitude. And Sasria said on 10 October, after violent protests in Soweto and Durban, that it is assessing the impact.

**Who wins, who loses:** insurers that treat unrest, weather and municipal service failure as one exposure to local government gain an early view of accumulation; insurers that keep them in separate desks do not. Results quoted this season, such as Discovery Insure's operating margin widening to 14.8%, describe the year to June, not the summer ahead.

## The Contrarian Take

**The contrarian claim:** the election outcome matters less to insurers than the capacity of the institutions it elects. The consensus treats 4 November as a political event, parks the insurance exposure at Sasria, and reads the Treasury's fiscal repair as proof that national risk has passed. The Treasury's own words describe the municipal tier differently, and the exposure sits across Sasria, property, crop and commercial books.

The strongest counter-argument deserves its due. Sasria says it is better prepared to process claims than in 2021, the fiscus has stabilised, all three rating agencies have taken positive actions this year, and SAWS cautions that a strong El Niño does not automatically bring drought. The election may well pass without systemic disruption. But the evidence is specific: transfers to 69 municipalities were suspended in June; police flagged 331 high-risk voting stations, more than half in KwaZulu-Natal; Business Day reported last November that Sasria's outlook lists unrest, water availability and infrastructure maintenance together; and the reforms for the municipal tier are still proposals.

**The strategic implication:** the unit of analysis should be the municipality, not the peril. The industry has no public, comparable view of where its unrest, weather and property exposures coincide, and the first insurers to build one will see accumulation before the claims reveal it.

## Closing Line

The Treasury has said where local government is weakest; the election, the weather and the claims will all arrive there together.
